In Room 1005, a Snack Platter Asks the Big Questions. Mostly, “Why Cucumbers?”

A dorm-room tasting menu in Evergreen C offers salami, ambition, a cheese-puff heist and a gastrointestinal epilogue no one requested.

By Isaiah Granet  ·  Who also ate it

There is a particular silence that falls over a room when a plate arrives that its creator believes in. I have heard it at Le Bernardin. I heard it again last week on the tenth floor of Evergreen C, Room 1005, when a platter was placed on a bed (there is no table; there is never a table) with the quiet confidence of a woman who had been to Trader Joe’s and had made choices.

Readers should know at the outset that this critic was not invited in any official capacity. I was invited as a boyfriend. That I arrived with a notebook, a palate, and a sense of grievance is a matter between me and my God.

“I want to be clear that I am completely objective,” said Mr. Granet, the critic, to this reviewer, who is also Mr. Granet. — A source close to the review

The Apples

We begin, as Eden did, with apples. They were sliced. They were crisp. They were, in the parlance of the young, “an apple.” Nothing about them was revolutionary, and that is precisely their point: the apple is the platter’s straight man, the reliable bass line over which the more flamboyant instruments will soon solo. I respect the apple. I do not think about the apple.

The Salami

Then the salami, fanned with the casual swagger of a deli counter that knows it has nothing to prove. Fatty, salty, faintly peppered. Paired with an apple slice it achieved a sweet-savory harmony that I can only describe as “Napa wedding, but in a dorm.” I ate a regrettable amount of it, a decision that, in retrospect, was strategically motivated by what was about to happen to the cheese puffs.

The Cucumbers

And then there were the cucumbers.

I have tried, in the days since, to find generosity. A cucumber, its defenders will say, is “refreshing.” So is a glass of water, and I do not ask a glass of water to sit beside salami and pretend it belongs. These rounds of pale, damp nothing lay on the platter like understudies who had wandered on stage during the wrong production. They contributed moisture. They contributed regret. They contributed, chiefly, the faint smell of a garden that has given up.

“I respect the cucumber as a concept,” Mr. Granet said. “I just don’t respect it as a food.” — The author, quoting himself, approvingly

The White Cheddar Puffs

If the cucumbers were the evening’s nadir, the Trader Joe’s white cheddar puffs were its summit, and, I am told, a spectacular one. I say “told” because I was largely unable to verify it personally. From the moment the bag was opened, my dining companion treated the puffs less as a shared course than as a sovereign territory. Her hand moved with the speed and precision of a heron. By the time I reached for my second puff, there was no second puff. There was only dust, and the sound of crunching from the other side of the bed.

What little I did taste was transcendent: airy, almost weightless, with a white-cheddar tang that clung to the fingertips like a beautiful memory, which is, sadly, all it is to me now.

“I would describe the puffs as extraordinary,” Mr. Granet said, “based on the one I had, and on the look on her face during the forty-five she had.” — The author, still processing

The Rocket Crackers

Then came the rocket-shaped cheddar crackers, whose official name the house declined to disclose and this reviewer was too busy eating to ask. Imagine a Goldfish that has read about Elon and chosen ambition. They were crunchy, aggressively cheesy, and shaped for liftoff, and they were, genuinely, very good. In a just world they would have been the star. In this world, they were the Ringo.

The Chocolate-Covered Pretzels

Dessert arrived without ceremony, because dessert was already on the platter, because this is a platter and not a coward. Chocolate-covered pretzels: salt, snap, sweetness, the eternal promise of American snacking. They closed the meal the way a good final paragraph closes an essay, by reminding you why you sat down in the first place.

The Aftermath

It would be journalistic malpractice not to report that, roughly an hour after service, my dining companion developed a stomach ache. The kitchen has offered no official explanation. This critic has his theories. I would note only that one of us ate approximately the entire bag of white cheddar puffs, and that one of us did not, and that only one of us was lying down afterward saying “ow.” Draw your own conclusions. I have drawn mine, and they are in a notebook.

“I am not saying it was the puffs,” Mr. Granet said. “I am saying the puffs know what they did.” — The author, from the scene

The Verdict

And yet. Here is what I cannot shake. The cucumbers were a misstep; the puffs were a robbery; the dining room was a bed. None of it mattered. The platter had heart. It had range. It had the courage to put salami next to a chocolate pretzel and trust you to figure it out. And it was made, start to finish, by someone who wanted to sit on the tenth floor of Evergreen C and eat snacks with me, which, it turns out, is the only ingredient a critic can’t source himself.

I will be back. I will bring my own cheese puffs. I will leave the cucumbers where they lie.

★★★★
Extraordinary.
Location
Evergreen C, Room 1005, Stanford University. Seating on the bed.
Price
Free, but the cheese puffs will cost you your dignity.
Atmosphere
Intimate. Dorm-lit. Occasional hallway noise.
Noise level
Conversational, punctuated by aggressive crunching.
Recommended
White cheddar puffs (if you are fast), rocket crackers, salami with apple, chocolate pretzels.
Avoid
Cucumbers. Reaching for puffs within striking distance of the chef.
Health notes
Pace yourself. One diner was briefly incapacitated.
